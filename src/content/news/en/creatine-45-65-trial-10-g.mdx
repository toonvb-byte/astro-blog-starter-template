## What happened

Researchers at Texas A&M University, led by long-time creatine researcher Richard Kreider, published a 12-week trial in the *Journal of the International Society of Sports Nutrition* (online August 2026; university press release 18 September). **64 healthy adults aged 45 to 65** either followed a structured exercise and weight-loss diet programme or did not. Within each group they were randomly given **10 g of creatine monohydrate a day** or a placebo. Who joined the exercise programme was not randomised.

- **Without the programme**, the creatine group gained about **1.1 kg of lean tissue** and improved in leg press and bench press tests compared with placebo. The authors also report favourable changes in some blood-fat measures and a lower HbA1c.
- **With exercise and diet**, adding creatine gave larger strength gains and a bigger drop in body fat percentage than the programme alone.

The study was funded by the WoodNext Foundation through the Texas A&M Foundation.

## What it means for you

- **Read "lean tissue" carefully.** It was measured by DXA, which counts water as well as muscle, and creatine pulls water into muscle cells. The trial was also small and short.
- **10 g is not the authorised dose.** The UK and EU claims use **3 g of creatine a day**; the claim for over-55s requires resistance training at least three times a week. 3 g of creatine means about 3.4 g of monohydrate.
- **Training still matters most.** The bigger gains came when creatine was added to exercise.
- **If you have kidney disease or take medicines**, ask your doctor before starting creatine. Creatine can also raise blood creatinine, which may look like a kidney problem in a routine test, so mention it before blood tests.
