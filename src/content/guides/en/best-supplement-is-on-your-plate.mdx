## The short answer

The healthiest way to take your vitamins and minerals is to eat them. That is not just our view: [Directive 2002/46/EC](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/dir/2002/46/oj) requires every food supplement sold in the EU to state on the label that it should not be used as a substitute for a varied diet, and Germany's BfR considers supplements generally unnecessary for healthy people who eat a varied, balanced diet. There are clear exceptions, which we cover below, but the starting point is your plate.

## Why food comes first

- **One plate covers several nutrients at once.** A serving of Spanish spinach with chickpeas gives about 170 mg of magnesium and 7.4 mg of iron: close to half the NRV of both.
- **It is hard to overdo it with ordinary food.** EFSA's upper limit for magnesium (250 mg a day) applies only to supplements, water and fortified foods, not to the magnesium naturally in food.
- **No small print.** With a supplement you have to check the form, the dose and the limit. With a bowl of lentils, you don't.

## What food can cover, nutrient by nutrient

| Nutrient | Covered by food? | With what | When to think about a supplement |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Magnesium | Yes | A handful of pumpkin seeds (165–178 mg), pulses, nuts, oats | If your doctor finds a low level |
| Iron | Yes, with some care | Pulses with vitamin C, meat, mussels | Deficiency confirmed by a blood test, pregnancy |
| Zinc | Yes | Red meat, shellfish, pulses, seeds, cheese | Vegan diets high in phytate, if your doctor advises |
| Vitamin C | Yes, easily | Half a raw red pepper (96 mg), broccoli, citrus | Hardly ever |
| Vitamin D | Only in part | Oily fish, eggs | Little sun, especially in winter, after a blood test |
| Vitamin B12 | Yes, if you eat animal products | Meat, fish, eggs, dairy | Always on a vegan diet |
| Folic acid | Folate from vegetables and pulses, yes | Leafy greens, pulses | When trying for a baby and up to week 12 |
| Omega-3 (EPA and DHA) | Yes, if you eat oily fish | Herring, mackerel, salmon, sardines | If you eat no fish: algae oil |
| Creatine | Only in part | Meat and fish: about 1 g per 450 g | For the 3 g a day sports effect |

## Behind the table

**Magnesium, zinc and vitamin C.** These are the easy ones. Half a raw red pepper gives 96 mg of vitamin C, and a 30 g handful of pumpkin seeds about 165–178 mg of magnesium, depending on whether they are roasted. EFSA sets an adequate magnesium intake of 350 mg a day for men and 300 mg for women. The details are in [magnesium-rich foods](/en/guides/magnesium-rich-foods/) and [vitamin C and zinc in winter foods](/en/guides/vitamin-c-and-zinc-foods/).

**Iron.** Pulses, meat and shellfish contain plenty, but the body absorbs only part of it: 14–18% from mixed diets and 5–12% from vegetarian diets, according to the NIH. Vitamin C in the same meal helps; we show how in [iron-rich foods](/en/guides/iron-rich-foods/). If you feel tired, read [iron supplements and tiredness](/en/guides/iron-supplements-tiredness/) before buying anything.

**Vitamin D.** In the NIH's words, "few foods naturally contain vitamin D". Our calculations show that even oily fish three times a week plus an egg a day covers at most about half of what EFSA considers adequate. The rest comes from sunlight. We work it out in [vitamin D foods](/en/guides/vitamin-d-foods/); the winter gap is covered in [vitamin D in winter](/en/guides/vitamin-d-winter-europe/).

**Vitamin B12.** If you eat meat, fish, eggs or dairy, food is usually enough. On a vegan diet it is not: the German Nutrition Society recommends a supplement. More in [who needs a B12 supplement](/en/guides/vitamin-b12-who-needs-supplement/).

**Folic acid.** The NHS recommends 400 micrograms of folic acid a day while you are trying to get pregnant, ideally from three months before, and for the first 12 weeks of pregnancy. Here food does not replace the supplement.

**Omega-3.** EFSA considers 250 mg of EPA and DHA a day adequate, and fatty fish is the main source. If you eat no fish, algae oil is the alternative: see [omega-3 fish oil vs algae oil](/en/guides/omega-3-fish-oil-vs-algae-oil/).

**Creatine.** According to researcher Richard Kreider (Texas A&M), a pound (450 g) of red meat or fish provides about 1 g of creatine, and the body makes about 1 g a day. Reaching the 3 g a day in the EU-authorised claim from food alone would take well over 1 kg of meat or fish a day. More in our [creatine guide](/en/guides/creatine-monohydrate-guide/).

## When a supplement does make sense

- **A blood test shows a deficiency** of iron, vitamin D or B12. Your doctor will set the dose and duration.
- **You are vegan:** B12 is not optional.
- **You are trying for a baby or are in the first 12 weeks of pregnancy:** folic acid.
- **You get very little sun:** food is unlikely to cover vitamin D.
- **Your doctor advises it** because of an illness, a medication or a specific situation.

Outside those cases, check your plate before you buy a jar.

## How to read our food tables

Each guide in this series gives:

- **Figures per real serving**, not per 100 g: a handful of seeds, a plate of pulses, a tin of sardines.
- **The % NRV**, the reference value EU labels use.
- **The EU legal threshold.** A food may be advertised as a "source of" a nutrient if it provides at least 15% of the NRV per 100 g, and as "high" in it from 30%.
- **Ranges** when official databases disagree, instead of a falsely precise number.
- **Calculated recipes** using the same data.

Values come from official food composition databases (USDA, Spain's BEDCA and France's CIQUAL), and NutriMetrics does the calculations.
