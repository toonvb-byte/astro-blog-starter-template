## The short answer

There is no single best magnesium. For most adults, a **citrate** or **bisglycinate** supplying 100–250 mg of elemental magnesium a day is a reasonable choice: both are absorbed better than oxide and are usually well tolerated. **Oxide** is the cheapest per tablet but is less well absorbed. "Brain" forms such as L-threonate contain very little magnesium per capsule and have little human evidence.

Before you compare prices, find two numbers on the label: **mg of elemental magnesium per daily dose** and **how many daily doses are in the pack**.

## How much real magnesium each form contains

Magnesium is always sold bound to another molecule, and that partner decides how much of each capsule is actually magnesium. A tub of "1,000 mg magnesium bisglycinate" can contain less magnesium than one labelled "400 mg magnesium citrate".

| Form                   | Elemental magnesium (approx.) | Compound needed for 200 mg magnesium | Usually chosen for                   |
| ---------------------- | ----------------------------- | ------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------ |
| Oxide                  | \~60%                         | \~330 mg                             | Lowest price per tablet              |
| Carbonate              | \~28%                         | \~710 mg                             | Cheap, often in effervescent tablets |
| Citrate                | \~16%                         | \~1,250 mg                           | Good absorption at a mid price       |
| Malate                 | \~15%                         | \~1,330 mg                           | Alternative to citrate               |
| Bisglycinate           | \~14%                         | \~1,430 mg                           | Gentle on the stomach                |
| Chloride (hexahydrate) | \~12%                         | \~1,670 mg                           | Common in powders and liquids        |
| L-threonate            | \~7%                          | \~2,860 mg                           | Marketed for memory; little evidence |

Percentages are calculated from each salt's molecular weight and vary with hydration. The label figure always wins.

## Which form is absorbed best

The clearest official summary is the **NIH Office of Dietary Supplements** fact sheet: forms that dissolve well in liquid are absorbed more completely. Small studies found **aspartate, citrate, lactate and chloride** more bioavailable than **oxide and sulfate**.

Bisglycinate is not part of that comparison. It is widely described as well absorbed and well tolerated, but there is little direct evidence comparing it with citrate.

Better absorption does not automatically mean a better outcome. If your diet includes nuts, legumes, whole grains and leafy greens, you may not need a supplement at all.

## What magnesium can claim in the EU

In the EU, a supplement can only make health claims listed in the official register, in the authorised wording. For magnesium the main ones are that it contributes to:

* the **reduction of tiredness and fatigue**;
* normal **muscle function** and normal functioning of the **nervous system**;
* normal **psychological function**;
* normal **energy-yielding metabolism**;
* the maintenance of normal **bones and teeth**.

A product must supply at least 15% of the reference intake (56 mg of magnesium per daily dose) to use them. There is **no authorised claim** for sleep, anxiety, cramps or migraine. Packs that promise those are going beyond what EU law allows.

## Maximum doses in Europe

The EU has never set a binding maximum for magnesium in supplements, so the limits differ by authority:

| Authority           | Limit for magnesium from supplements      | Status                        |
| ------------------- | ----------------------------------------- | ----------------------------- |
| EFSA (EU)           | 250 mg/day for adults                     | Scientific safe upper level   |
| BfR (Germany)       | 250 mg/day, split into at least two doses | Recommendation                |
| Spain               | No national maximum                       | EU rules apply                |
| NIH (United States) | 350 mg/day                                | US figure, not used in the EU |

The effect behind EFSA's limit is diarrhoea. According to the NIH, **carbonate, chloride and oxide** are the forms most often linked to it. Our guide to [supplement maximum levels in Europe](/en/guides/supplement-maximum-levels-europe/) covers other nutrients country by country.

One product in our database shows why this matters: natural elements *Magnesium Bisglycinat*, sold in Germany, lists **300 mg of magnesium in 3 capsules**. That is above the BfR recommendation. It is not illegal, and taking 2 capsules brings it to 200 mg.

**Talk to a doctor or pharmacist before taking magnesium** if you have kidney disease, or take bisphosphonates, tetracycline or quinolone antibiotics, diuretics, or proton pump inhibitors long term.

## Which one to choose

| Situation               | Form usually chosen | Why                                               |
| ----------------------- | ------------------- | ------------------------------------------------- |
| Sensitive stomach       | Bisglycinate        | Usually well tolerated at moderate doses          |
| Tight budget            | Citrate             | Good absorption for less money than bisglycinate  |
| Lowest price per tablet | Oxide               | Most magnesium per tablet, but absorbed less well |
| Powder or liquid        | Citrate or chloride | Dissolve well                                     |

## How to compare prices properly

Divide the pack price by the number of daily doses, then by the mg of magnesium in each dose:

**pack price ÷ daily doses per pack ÷ mg magnesium per dose × 100 = cost per 100 mg**

For the example above: €23.99 ÷ 60 daily doses = €0.40 a day for 300 mg, or about **€0.13 per 100 mg** of magnesium (indicative price at shop-apotheke.com, checked 15 October 2026). This lets you compare a 60-tablet pack with a 180-capsule tub on equal terms.
