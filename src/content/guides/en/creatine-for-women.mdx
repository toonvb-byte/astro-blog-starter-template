## The short answer

If you do strength training or sports with short, intense efforts, creatine works the same in women as in men: it is the best-studied sports supplement and has EU-authorised health claims. Take **3–5 g of monohydrate a day**. What social media says about menopause, bones or memory is **ahead of the evidence**: some studies are promising but small, and some results have been negative.

## What is proven

The EU authorises two claims for creatine, and neither distinguishes by sex:

| Authorised claim | For whom | Condition |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Increases physical performance in successive bursts of short-term, high-intensity exercise | Adults doing high-intensity exercise | 3 g of creatine a day |
| Can enhance the effect of resistance training on muscle strength | Adults over 55 doing regular resistance training | 3 g a day plus training at least 3 times a week |

The second claim is particularly relevant for women after menopause, when muscle mass declines with age. But the condition is clear: **daily creatine plus resistance training**. EFSA found that taking it only on training days did not give the same result.

A 2021 review of creatine in women concluded that effects on strength and performance are similar to those in men and that it is well tolerated across life stages.

## Menopause, bones and brain: what is not yet known

| Topic | Key study | Result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bone density | 2-year trial in **237 postmenopausal women**, creatine plus exercise (2023) | **No improvement** in hip bone density. More lean mass, faster walking and some preserved measures of bone geometry |
| Cognition in menopause | CONCRET-MENOPA trial, **36 women**, 8 weeks (2025) | Better scores on some cognitive tests and higher brain creatine. Used **creatine HCl**, not monohydrate; the manufacturer supplied the product |
| Performance and muscle across life stages | 2025 review | Possible benefits at 3–5 g a day, mainly **combined with exercise** |

The honest conclusion: creatine is a reasonable tool to **maintain muscle and strength** if you train. For bones, the largest trial so far was negative. For memory or menopause symptoms, larger studies are needed. There are **no authorised claims** for those uses, so a product sold "for menopause" may not promise them.

## Common myths

**"It makes you fat."** Creatine adds no meaningful calories. In the first weeks you may gain around 1 kg, especially with loading: that is water inside the muscle, not fat. Without loading, the change is gradual.

**"It makes your face or stomach puffy."** The water is stored inside muscle fibres, not under the skin. If your stomach is upset, splitting the dose usually helps.

**"It makes you bulky."** Creatine helps you train harder; body composition changes depend on training and diet, just as without it.

**"It's for men."** Neither the EU claims nor the evidence distinguish by sex.

## How to take it

1. **Form:** monohydrate — the best-studied and cheapest. More in our [creatine monohydrate guide](/en/guides/creatine-monohydrate-guide/).
2. **Dose:** 3–5 g a day. Reaching the 3 g of creatine in the EU claim takes about 3.4 g of monohydrate.
3. **Consistency:** every day, rest days included. Timing matters little.
4. **Loading:** optional. Without it, muscles are saturated in about 4 weeks.
5. **With training:** almost all benefits appear when combined with exercise, especially strength training.

**Talk to your doctor** before taking it if you are pregnant or breastfeeding, or have kidney disease. Creatine raises creatinine in blood tests, so mention it before a test.
