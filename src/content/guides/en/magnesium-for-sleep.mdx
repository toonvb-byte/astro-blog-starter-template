## The short answer

Magnesium **may help a little** with sleep, especially if your diet is low in it, but the trials are small and the effects modest. EU law **does not allow** any brand to claim that magnesium improves sleep. If you want to try it, take **bisglycinate or citrate**, **up to 250 mg of magnesium a day** from the supplement, for at least **4 weeks**, and judge whether it makes a difference. If you have slept badly for more than three months, see a doctor: chronic insomnia has treatments with far stronger evidence.

## What the studies show

| Study                                 | Who took part                                                              | Result                                                                                                                |
| ------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Mah & Pitre meta-analysis (2021)      | 3 trials, older adults with insomnia                                       | Fell asleep about **17 minutes sooner** than with placebo; no clear change in total sleep time. Evidence quality: low |
| Schuster et al. trial (2025)          | 155 adults reporting poor sleep, 4 weeks, 250 mg magnesium as bisglycinate | **Small** improvement in insomnia severity vs placebo (d = 0.2, p = 0.049). No difference in fatigue, stress or mood  |
| Rawji et al. systematic review (2024) | 8 sleep studies                                                            | 5 positive, 1 mixed, 2 no effect. Possible help for mild insomnia, mainly with low magnesium                          |

The pattern is consistent: a **small effect**, measured mostly by questionnaires, in **short and small** studies. The authors of the 2025 trial call the improvement modest and ask for studies with objective sleep measurements.

## What EU law allows on the label

Magnesium has several authorised claims: it contributes to the **reduction of tiredness and fatigue**, normal **nervous system** and **muscle** function, and normal **psychological function**. None mentions sleep.

The **only** authorised sleep claim belongs to **melatonin**: it contributes to reducing the time taken to fall asleep, at 1 mg shortly before bedtime. That is why many "sleep" products combine magnesium with melatonin. A magnesium-only pack promising "better sleep" is going beyond what EU law allows. See our [table of EU-approved health claims](/en/guides/eu-approved-health-claims-supplements/).

## How to take it if you want to try

1. **Form:** bisglycinate or citrate. Both are absorbed better than oxide and usually well tolerated. More in our guide to [magnesium forms](/en/guides/magnesium-forms-compared/).
2. **Dose:** 100–250 mg of **elemental** magnesium a day. EFSA's upper level for magnesium from supplements is 250 mg; above that, diarrhoea becomes more likely.
3. **Timing:** afternoon or evening, as in the studies. Germany's BfR advises splitting the daily amount into at least two doses.
4. **Duration:** at least 4 weeks before deciding whether it works.
5. **Check your diet:** nuts, legumes, whole grains, cocoa and leafy greens all supply magnesium. If you already eat plenty, a supplement probably adds little.

**Talk to a doctor or pharmacist first** if you have kidney disease, or take bisphosphonates, tetracycline or quinolone antibiotics, diuretics or proton pump inhibitors long term.

## If magnesium is not enough

Supplements do not replace what works best for sleep: regular sleep times, daylight in the morning, less caffeine after midday and fewer screens before bed. For chronic insomnia, cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) is the first-line treatment in the 2023 European Insomnia Guideline.
