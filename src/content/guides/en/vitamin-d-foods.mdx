## The short answer

Only in part. The NIH puts it simply: "few foods naturally contain vitamin D". Oily fish is the big exception, and a serving of mackerel or horse mackerel can cover the 15 µg a day that EFSA considers adequate. But eating like that every day isn't realistic. According to the NIH, most people meet at least some of their vitamin D needs through sunlight. If you get little sun in winter, food alone rarely covers it; we explain more in [vitamin D in winter](/en/guides/vitamin-d-winter-europe/).

## How much vitamin D you need

EFSA sets an adequate intake of **15 µg (600 IU) a day** for adults. Note that it is calculated on the assumption that the skin makes very little vitamin D, in other words for someone who gets almost no sun. Labels show an NRV of 5 µg, which is much lower; that is why our table uses EFSA's 15 µg as the reference.

## How much each serving gives you

Food composition databases disagree on the vitamin D in fish. We give the range across the databases that list each food: CIQUAL (France), BEDCA (Spain) and USDA (United States).

| Food | Serving | Vitamin D (µg) | % of 15 µg | Databases |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Mackerel | 125 g | 8.1–20.1 | 54–134% | CIQUAL, BEDCA, USDA |
| Horse mackerel (scad) | 125 g | 20.0 | 133% | BEDCA |
| Salmon, farmed | 125 g | 4.6–13.8 | 31–92% | CIQUAL, BEDCA, USDA |
| Cod liver oil | 1 tsp (4.5 g) | 9.4–11.2 | 63–75% | BEDCA, CIQUAL, USDA |
| Sardines, fresh | 125 g | 10.0 | 67% | BEDCA |
| Anchovies, fresh | 125 g | 10.0 | 67% | BEDCA |
| Tuna, fresh | 125 g | 9.0–9.8 | 60–65% | BEDCA, CIQUAL |
| Trout | 125 g | 2.6–7.4 | 18–49% | BEDCA, CIQUAL |
| Sardines in oil, drained | 1 tin (60 g) | 2.9–4.9 | 19–33% | BEDCA, USDA |
| Tuna in water, tinned | 1 tin (60 g) | 0.7–3.0 | 5–20% | BEDCA, CIQUAL, USDA |
| Egg | 1 medium (60 g) | 1.0–1.2 | 7–8% | BEDCA, CIQUAL, USDA |
| Hard cheese (aged) | 30 g | 0.3 | 2% | BEDCA |
| Mushrooms, ordinary | 100 g | 0.0–0.2 | 0–1% | BEDCA, USDA |
| Whole milk, not fortified | 1 glass (250 ml) | 0.1 | 0% | BEDCA |

**What the table shows:**

- **Fresh oily fish** is the only everyday food that can cover the day in one serving. Mackerel, horse mackerel, sardines and anchovies are at the top.
- **Tinned fish gives less.** A tin of sardines gives 3–5 µg and a tin of tuna in water 1–3 µg.
- **An egg** adds about 1 µg: it helps, but it isn't enough.
- **Ordinary mushrooms and unfortified milk** give almost nothing. The NIH notes that some mushrooms are treated with UV light to increase their vitamin D2, and some milks and cereals have vitamin D added: the label will tell you.

## A week with oily fish: is it enough?

We calculated a week with three servings of oily fish and an egg a day:

| Meal | Times a week | Vitamin D |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Farmed salmon (125 g) | 1 | 4.6–13.8 µg |
| Mackerel (125 g) | 1 | 8.1–20.1 µg |
| Fresh sardines (125 g) | 1 | 10.0 µg |
| Egg (60 g) | 7 | 7.1–8.4 µg |
| **Average per day** | | **4.3–7.5 µg (28–50% of 15 µg)** |

That is a better diet than average, and it still covers half the adequate intake at most. The rest has to come from the sun, or from a supplement if a blood test shows you are low.

## Two recipes with oily fish

One comes from our Spanish edition, one from our German edition.

### Grilled mackerel with roasted peppers (2 servings)

**Ingredients:** 2 mackerel, filleted (about 250 g of fish), 2 roasted red peppers, olive oil, garlic, salt and a few drops of sherry vinegar.

1. Pat the fillets dry and cook them on a very hot griddle, skin side first, 2–3 minutes a side.
2. Serve with the peppers cut into strips and dressed with oil, chopped garlic and vinegar.

**Per serving:** between **8.1 and 20.1 µg of vitamin D** (54–134% of 15 µg), depending on the database.

### Oven-baked salmon with potatoes and broccoli (2 servings)

**Ingredients:** 2 salmon fillets of 125 g, 400 g potatoes, 300 g broccoli, 1 lemon, 2 tbsp rapeseed oil, salt and pepper.

1. Cut the potatoes into wedges, toss with oil and salt and bake at 200 °C for 20 minutes.
2. Add the salmon and broccoli florets, squeeze over the lemon and bake for 12 more minutes.

**Per serving:** between **4.6 and 13.8 µg of vitamin D** (31–92% of 15 µg) for farmed salmon.

## When food isn't enough

- **If you get little sun**, because of work, the season or covered skin, food is unlikely to cover your vitamin D.
- **The only way to know** is a 25(OH)D blood test. If the result is low, your doctor will tell you what dose to take. Compare products on our [vitamin D](/en/supplements/vitamin-d/) page.
- **Cod liver oil** is rich in vitamin D, but also in vitamin A. The NHS advises not taking it during pregnancy.

## How we calculated the numbers

For each food we looked up the value per 100 g in three official databases: CIQUAL (ANSES, France), BEDCA (AESAN, Spain) and FoodData Central SR Legacy (USDA, United States). Where they disagree, we give the lowest and highest values; where only one database lists the food, we give that value. NutriMetrics calculates the servings, percentages and the example week. The real vitamin D content of fish varies from one fish to the next.
