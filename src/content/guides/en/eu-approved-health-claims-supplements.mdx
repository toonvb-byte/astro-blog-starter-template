faq:
  - q: What is a health claim?
    a: >-
      Any statement that suggests a relationship between a food or ingredient
      and health, such as "contributes to normal muscle function". Under
      Regulation (EC) No 1924/2006, which the UK kept after Brexit, only claims
      on the Great Britain register may be used in England, Scotland and Wales,
      and only claims on the EU register in Northern Ireland and the EU.
  - q: Why does a collagen product mention vitamin C?
    a: >-
      Collagen itself has no authorised claim. Vitamin C does: it contributes to
      normal collagen formation for the normal function of skin, cartilage and
      bones. Adding enough vitamin C lets the product use that claim legally.
  - q: Can a supplement say it prevents a disease?
    a: >-
      No. Food supplements may not claim to prevent, treat or cure any disease;
      in the UK, a product that makes medicinal claims is treated as a medicine
      and needs a licence from the MHRA.
      A handful of "disease risk reduction" claims exist under strict
      conditions, such as folic acid and neural tube defects, but they are the
      exception.
  - q: What does "on hold" mean for botanical claims?
    a: >-
      In 2010 the European Commission paused the evaluation of claims for plants
      and plant extracts, which make up the large majority of the claims still
      pending. Great Britain took over the same on-hold list in 2021. None has
      been authorised since, so a botanical product cannot rely on an authorised
      claim.
  - q: Where can I check a claim myself?
    a: >-
      For Great Britain, in the GB nutrition and health claims register, a
      spreadsheet published by the Department of Health and Social Care on
      GOV.UK. For Northern Ireland and the EU, in the EU Register run by the
      European Commission. Both list authorised claims with their conditions of
      use and rejected claims.
  - q: Will UK and EU claims stay the same?
    a: >-
      So far they have barely diverged. The UK and EU are negotiating a
      sanitary and phytosanitary (SPS) agreement, targeted for mid-2027, under
      which Great Britain would follow EU rules on nutrition and health claims
      and food supplements again, including future changes.

## The short answer

Most supplement packs in the UK and the EU can only say a small number of things, and only in set words. **Vitamins and minerals** have dozens of authorised claims. A few other ingredients — **omega-3 DHA and EPA, creatine, melatonin, glucomannan** — have one or two each. **Plants and extracts** (ashwagandha, turmeric, valerian, rhodiola, milk thistle) have **none**, and nor do glucosamine, probiotics or collagen on their own.

When a pack promises more than the claims in the table below, it is going beyond what the law allows.

## UK and EU: one table, two registers

* **Great Britain** (England, Scotland, Wales) uses the **GB nutrition and health claims register**, kept by the Department of Health and Social Care. New applications are assessed by the UK Nutrition and Health Claims Committee (UKNHCC) instead of EFSA.
* **Northern Ireland** still uses the **EU register**, because EU food law applies there under the Windsor Framework.
* **The content is the same for everything in this guide.** The GB register was copied from the EU register on 1 January 2021. The decisions GB has taken on its own since then are all rejections or narrow changes: a lutein and zeaxanthin combination, a change to a beta-glucan claim, **creatine and cognitive function** (rejected in 2024 because the evidence for 3 g a day did not show an effect) and green kiwifruit powder (2026). The European Commission has also moved to reject the same creatine claim (draft regulation notified to the WTO in 2025).
* **Adverts too.** In the UK, the Advertising Standards Authority applies the same register to ads and websites (CAP Code section 15).
* **What may change:** under the UK-EU SPS agreement being negotiated for mid-2027, Great Britain would align with EU rules on claims and food supplements again.

## How the rules work

* **The register is the list.** Regulation (EC) No 1924/2006, kept in UK law after Brexit, says a health claim may only be used if it is authorised and listed in the register. Most general claims are in Regulation (EU) No 432/2012.
* **The wording is fixed.** Brands may adapt the phrasing slightly for consumers, but the meaning must stay the same. "Contributes to the reduction of tiredness and fatigue" cannot become "beats fatigue".
* **There is a minimum dose.** For vitamins and minerals, the product must be at least a "source" of the nutrient: **15% of the nutrient reference value** per daily dose. Other ingredients have their own conditions (3 g of creatine, 1 mg of melatonin, 250 mg of DHA).
* **No disease claims.** A supplement may not claim to prevent, treat or cure a disease. In the UK, a product presented that way is treated as a medicine by the MHRA.

## Ingredients with no authorised claims

| Ingredient                                                                                     | Why there is no claim                                                                                                                     |
| ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Ashwagandha, turmeric/curcumin, valerian, rhodiola, milk thistle, ginseng and other botanicals | Claims for plants have been "on hold" since 2010 and never authorised                                                                     |
| Glucosamine and chondroitin                                                                    | Joint claims assessed by EFSA and rejected                                                                                                |
| Probiotics                                                                                     | All claims for specific strains assessed so far have been rejected. In the UK, the Department of Health treats the word "probiotic" itself as a health claim, so it should not appear on labels; some EU countries now allow it |
| Collagen                                                                                       | No authorised claim of its own; products rely on added vitamin C                                                                          |
| Caffeine in supplements                                                                        | No claim authorised for food supplements                                                                                                  |

This is why so many collagen, joint and "immune" products contain a vitamin or mineral: it is the only way to make a legal claim on the pack. See our [ashwagandha guide](/en/guides/ashwagandha-eu-legal-status/) for an example of a botanical with no claim and growing regulatory scrutiny.

## Disease risk reduction claims

A small number of claims may mention reducing a disease **risk factor**, under strict conditions. The best-known one for supplements is **folic acid**: supplemental folic acid increases maternal folate status, and low maternal folate is a risk factor for neural tube defects in the developing foetus. The claim applies to products providing at least 400 µg of folic acid a day, taken from at least one month before conception until three months after. The NHS advises 400 µg a day before pregnancy and until 12 weeks, and a higher 5 mg dose on prescription for women at higher risk.

## How to use this table when you shop

1. **Read the claim, then the dose.** A "tiredness and fatigue" claim on a magnesium product is only valid if the daily dose contains at least 56 mg of magnesium.
2. **Spot the borrowed claim.** If a collagen, ashwagandha or turmeric product carries a claim, check which added vitamin or mineral it comes from.
3. **Treat anything else as marketing.** Phrases like "supports sleep", "detox", "boosts testosterone" or "for healthy joints" with no matching nutrient have no authorised basis.
