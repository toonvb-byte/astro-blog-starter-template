Bones, muscle and the way your gut handles vitamin B12 all change over time, and this is the age when many people start asking which supplements, if any, are worth taking. The honest answer is that a supplement rarely replaces a varied diet, and the right choice depends on your health, your diet and your medicines.

## What the EU lets supplements say

The EU register of authorised health claims is the only list that counts for labels in the EU. For the nutrients on this page, the claims that are authorised are these:

- **Calcium:** "Calcium is needed for the maintenance of normal bones" and "Calcium is needed for the maintenance of normal teeth".
- **Vitamin D:** "Vitamin D contributes to the maintenance of normal bones", "Vitamin D contributes to the maintenance of normal muscle function" and "Vitamin D contributes to the normal function of the immune system".
- **Vitamin B12:** "Vitamin B12 contributes to normal red blood cell formation" and "Vitamin B12 contributes to normal functioning of the nervous system".
- **Protein:** "Protein contributes to the maintenance of muscle mass" and "Protein contributes to the maintenance of normal bones".

Each claim may be made only when the product is at least a source of the nutrient, as set out in Regulation (EC) No 1924/2006. Any other wording, such as a promise to prevent osteoporosis, is not authorised and should be treated as a warning sign.

## Vitamin D: the dose and the sun

EFSA sets an adequate intake of **15 µg (600 IU) a day** for adults. The NIH recommends 15 µg a day for people aged 51 to 70 and 20 µg (800 IU) from age 71. The NIH sets the **Tolerable Upper Intake at 100 µg (4,000 IU) a day** for adults, so a supplement that goes well above that is not a safe default.

Few foods naturally contain vitamin D. The NIH notes that most people get some of their vitamin D from sunlight, but UVB rays do not pass through window glass, so sun exposure indoors does not count. The NIH describes sun exposure as possible during spring, summer and autumn; it does not give a figure for winter, so do not assume a winter dose from sunlight alone. For winter, see our [vitamin D guide for winter in Europe](/en/guides/vitamin-d-winter-europe/) and the [vitamin D hub](/en/supplements/vitamin-d/).

## Vitamin B12: why absorption changes

The NIH explains that atrophic gastritis, which affects 8–9% of adults aged 65 and over, reduces the production of intrinsic factor and stomach acid. Both are needed to absorb B12 from food. The adult RDA is 2.4 µg a day. Because the body absorbs only a small share of very large single doses, labels often show high numbers; the [B12 guide](/en/guides/vitamin-b12-who-needs-supplement/) explains how to read them.

Three common medicines can reduce B12 absorption, according to the NIH:

- **Metformin**, which can significantly reduce serum B12.
- **Proton pump inhibitors** such as omeprazole and lansoprazole, and **H2 blockers** such as cimetidine and ranitidine, which slow the release of stomach acid.

If you take any of these, ask your doctor whether to test your B12 before you start a supplement. Do not change a prescription on your own.

## Protein and muscle

The EU-authorised protein claims concern muscle mass and bones. The NIH and EFSA guidance on protein intake for older adults is not summarised here because we did not check the figures in this draft; the [creatine guide for over-55s](/en/guides/creatine-after-55/) covers the one muscle supplement with the best evidence for that age group, with its own caveats. Good protein sources are usually food: dairy, eggs, fish, pulses and meat.

## Before you buy

Choose a product whose label shows the form and amount per daily dose, and check it against the NRV table in the [how to read an EU supplement label guide](/en/guides/how-to-read-eu-supplement-label/). If you take any prescription medicine, check possible interactions with your doctor or pharmacist before starting a new supplement. Interactions beyond the three medicine groups above were not checked against a source for this draft.

This article is general information, not advice for your own case. A doctor, pharmacist or registered dietitian can look at your blood tests, diet and medicines.
