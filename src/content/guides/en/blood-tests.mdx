## The short answer

A blood test measures one thing on one day. For vitamin D, B12 and iron it helps to know whether you are low at all, and a number can only be read in context: your symptoms, your diet, your medicines and any other results. Take the report to your GP, a pharmacist or a dietitian, and ask what it means before you buy or stop anything.

## Before the test: tell the lab what you take

Some supplements can change test results. The US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) warns that **biotin**, often found in hair and skin supplements, can significantly interfere with certain lab tests and cause incorrect results that may go undetected. Its troponin page reports falsely low troponin results, which are used to diagnose heart attacks. Tell the lab and your doctor about any biotin, including hair products, and when you last took it. Our [biotin guide](/en/guides/biotin-for-hair-loss/) explains the dose question.

Creatine affects one test: creatinine (see below). Other supplements mostly matter for the reason you are being tested, so bring a full list.

## Vitamin D: the 25(OH)D test

The test is 25-hydroxyvitamin D, written 25(OH)D. The NIH says it is the best indicator of vitamin D status, though it does not show how much is stored in your body. The NIH ranges are:

- **Below 30 nmol/L (below 12 ng/mL):** associated with deficiency.
- **30 to 50 nmol/L (12 to 20 ng/mL):** generally considered inadequate.
- **50 nmol/L (20 ng/mL) or more:** generally considered adequate.
- **Above 125 nmol/L (50 ng/mL):** emerging evidence links it to possible adverse effects.

The NIH gives a reference intake (RDA) of **15 µg (600 IU)** a day for adults up to 70, and **20 µg** over 70. The NHS advises everyone to consider **10 µg** a day in autumn and winter. Few foods contain vitamin D naturally, so most people depend on sunlight, supplements or fortified foods. For the winter picture in Europe, see [vitamin D in winter](/en/guides/vitamin-d-winter-europe/).

## Vitamin B12: the level and the next test

Most laboratories treat a serum B12 below **200 or 250 pg/mL** (148 or 185 pmol/L) as subnormal. The NIH notes the cut-off varies by method and laboratory. Between **150 and 399 pg/mL** (111 to 294 pmol/L), experts suggest measuring **methylmalonic acid (MMA)** as well: an MMA above **0.271 µmol/L** suggests deficiency. The NIH also notes that MMA rises with kidney problems and tends to be higher in older adults. Homocysteine above 15 µmol/L can suggest deficiency, but it is not specific, because folate and kidney function affect it.

The NHS says a GP can often diagnose B12 or folate deficiency anaemia from your symptoms and blood tests. The tests look at your haemoglobin, the size of your red blood cells, and the levels of B12 and folate. If the cause is unclear, the GP may order tests for pernicious anaemia, which are not always conclusive. For who may need a supplement, see [vitamin B12: who needs a supplement](/en/guides/vitamin-b12-who-needs-supplement/).

## Ferritin: the iron store test

Ferritin is the protein that stores iron, and it is the usual test for low iron stores. Its cut-offs and why it is read alongside haemoglobin are explained in [iron and tiredness: when an iron supplement actually helps](/en/guides/iron-supplements-tiredness/). Do not start iron on the strength of symptoms alone, because too much iron can also harm.

## Creatinine: after creatine

Creatine is broken down into creatinine. Doctors use blood creatinine to estimate kidney function, so creatine can raise the number without any change in your kidneys. Our [creatine guides](/en/guides/creatine-monohydrate-guide/) say the same. The best evidence we found is small and short. In one placebo-controlled trial, 18 healthy, sedentary men aged 18 to 35 took about **10 g of creatine a day for three months**. Serum creatinine did not change with training in the creatine group, while it fell in the placebo group. That trial does not settle the question for people with kidney disease, older adults or people on kidney-active medicines, so ask first.

## What to ask your doctor

- Is my result within the reference range on this report, and what unit does the lab use?
- If my B12 is in the borderline range, should we also check MMA?
- Does my result mean I need a supplement, or does it need a different investigation first?
- I take creatine (or biotin). Should I tell the lab, pause before the test, or ask for a cystatin C or eGFR result as well?
- If I start a supplement, what dose is sensible for me, and when should we test again?

This guide is general information, not advice for your own case. A doctor, pharmacist or dietitian can look at your results, medicines and history.
