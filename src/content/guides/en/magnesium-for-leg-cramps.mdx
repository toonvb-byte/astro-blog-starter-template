## The short answer

If you are an adult with ordinary night-time calf cramps, magnesium **probably won't help much more than a placebo**. That is the conclusion of the best evidence available, a 2020 Cochrane review. In **pregnancy** the picture is less clear: some trials saw fewer cramps, others didn't. For **cramps during sport** nobody has run a good trial. What reliably helps is cheaper: **stretch the calf**, every evening and during a cramp, and check whether a medicine or another condition is behind it.

## What the trials found

| Who | Evidence | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Adults with night cramps, mostly over 60 | Cochrane 2020: 5 trials, 271 people | Differences against placebo were small and not significant for frequency, intensity or duration. Moderate certainty |
| Pregnant women | Cochrane 2020 (pregnancy review): 8 small trials, 576 women | Inconsistent: one trial found more women free of cramps, others little or no difference. Low to very low certainty |
| People with exercise cramps | No randomised trials found | No evidence either way |
| Adults with night cramps (magnesium oxide, 4 weeks) | Roguin Maor 2017, 94 people | Cramps fell in both groups; no difference between magnesium and placebo |
| Adults over 45 with night cramps (one brand of magnesium oxide, 60 days) | Barna 2021, 175 people | 3.4 fewer cramps a week with magnesium vs 2.6 fewer with placebo |

Two things stand out. First, **placebo works surprisingly well** on cramps: in both recent trials the placebo group also had far fewer cramps. That is why your own experience ("I took magnesium and the cramps stopped") is not proof: cramps come and go on their own. Second, the one recent positive trial tested a single branded product and found a difference of **less than one cramp a week**. It is a reason for more research, not a reason to expect magnesium to fix your cramps.

## What a label is allowed to say

Magnesium has several authorised EU health claims. The one closest to cramps is **"magnesium contributes to normal muscle function"**. A product can use it from 15 % of the reference intake, which is about **56 mg of magnesium per daily dose**. The claim describes normal function, not prevention or treatment. A supplement that promises "against cramps" or "for cramp-free nights" goes beyond what the law allows. Our [table of authorised claims](/en/guides/eu-approved-health-claims-supplements/) has the exact wording of the magnesium claims.

## What helps instead

- **During a cramp:** straighten the leg and pull your toes up towards you, or stand and put weight on the foot. Massage the muscle.
- **To prevent cramps:** stretch your calves every day, especially before bed. The NHS suggests regular calf stretches; they may not stop cramps completely, but they can make them less frequent.
- **Look for a cause.** Cramps are more common with age, late in pregnancy, after hard exercise in heat, with some liver diseases and with some medicines, such as **statins and diuretics**. If cramps began after a new medicine, ask your doctor or pharmacist. Don't stop it on your own.

**See a doctor if** cramps disturb your sleep, last longer than 10 minutes, come with swelling or numbness, or your leg muscles are getting weaker or thinner.

## If you still want to try magnesium

Magnesium is cheap and safe at normal doses, so a short trial is reasonable as long as you keep it honest:

1. **Dose:** no more than **250 mg of magnesium a day from supplements**, the EFSA upper level. Above it, diarrhoea becomes more likely. Count the magnesium in any multivitamin too. Our [maximum levels by country](/en/guides/supplement-maximum-levels-europe/) show national limits.
2. **Form:** citrate or bisglycinate are gentler on the stomach than oxide. Read the label for **elemental magnesium per day**, not the weight of the compound. See [magnesium forms compared](/en/guides/magnesium-forms-compared/).
3. **Time limit:** the trials lasted 4 to 8 weeks. Write down how many cramps you have a week before you start and after a month. No change? Stop.
4. **Check first** with your doctor if you have kidney disease, are pregnant, or take antibiotics (tetracyclines, quinolones), bisphosphonates or long-term stomach-acid medicines.

Most adults already get a fair amount of magnesium from nuts, seeds, beans, whole grains and leafy greens. Our guide to [magnesium-rich foods](/en/guides/magnesium-rich-foods/) shows how much is in a normal day of eating.
