## The short answer

A vegan or vegetarian diet can meet every nutritional need, but it needs a plan for five nutrients. **Vitamin B12** is not found naturally in plant foods, so vegans need fortified foods or a supplement. **Iron, zinc and iodine** are present in plant foods, but the body absorbs less of them. **Omega-3 DHA** comes from fish in most diets, so vegans need algae oil or another plant source. Our [food-first guide](/en/guides/best-supplement-is-on-your-plate/) explains the general principle: start with the plate, then add a supplement only where food falls short.

## Vitamin B12: the non-negotiable one

The NHS says vegans may not get enough B12 and that it is not found naturally in plant foods. Vegetarians who eat eggs and dairy are usually covered, but those who eat few of either are not. Fortified plant milks, breakfast cereals and yeast extracts can help, provided you check the label. Older adults and people taking metformin or acid-reducing medicines absorb less B12, so ask your GP for a test. Our [B12 guide](/en/guides/vitamin-b12-who-needs-supplement/) covers the forms and labels in more detail.

## Iron: absorbed less, so pair it well

The NHS sets adult men's and women's aged 50 and over needs at 8.7 mg a day and women aged 19 to 49 at 14.8 mg a day. A serving of 150 g cooked lentils gives about 5 mg of iron, but the NIH says the body absorbs 14–18% of iron from a mixed diet and only 5–12% from a vegetarian one. Vitamin C in the same meal increases absorption, and tea, coffee and calcium-rich foods reduce it. An authorised EU claim says iron contributes to the reduction of tiredness and fatigue. See [iron-rich foods](/en/guides/iron-rich-foods/) for the numbers per serving.

## Iodine: depends on salt and dairy

The reference intake for adults is 150 µg a day. The BfR estimates that adults get about 75 µg a day from food without iodised salt, and that the supply in Germany is falling as manufacturers use less iodised salt. Vegans and people who avoid milk and fish are at higher risk. Iodised salt is the simplest fix. Dried seaweed should not be used as a reliable source: the BfR warns that a gram or two of dried kombu can supply several thousand micrograms, far above the 600 µg a day it considers a safe limit.

## Zinc: phytates reduce absorption

The NHS puts adult zinc needs at 9.5 mg a day for men and 7 mg for women. EFSA gives a wider range, 9.4 to 16.3 mg for men and 7.5 to 12.7 mg for women, depending on how much phytate the diet contains. Phytates are found in whole grains and pulses, and they bind zinc. Six oysters give 13 mg, but most plant foods give much less per serving. The NHS says not to take more than 25 mg a day of zinc from supplements, and the NIH warns that long-term high doses can lower copper. More in [vitamin C and zinc foods](/en/guides/vitamin-c-and-zinc-foods/).

## Omega-3: where DHA comes from

EFSA considers 250 mg a day of EPA and DHA adequate, and the authorised claims for heart function (EPA and DHA) and brain and vision (DHA) use that amount. Algae oil is the vegan source of DHA. A 2013 trial found it raised blood DHA to similar levels as fish oil. Many algae oils contain mostly DHA with little EPA, so compare the labels. The BfR advises people with heart disease to speak to a doctor before taking omega-3 preparations at high doses. Our [omega-3 comparison](/en/guides/omega-3-fish-oil-vs-algae-oil/) has the details.

## Medicines and timing

Some combinations need planning. Calcium, iron and magnesium can block levothyroxine unless you take them about 4 hours apart. Iron, zinc and magnesium can reduce the effect of some antibiotics. Metformin and proton pump inhibitors lower B12 absorption. The full timing table is in [which supplements not to take together](/en/guides/which-supplements-not-to-take-together/). If you take any prescribed medicine, ask your pharmacist before adding a supplement.

## When to get a test

A blood test is the only reliable way to know whether you are low. Ask your GP about B12 and ferritin, the marker for iron stores, if you feel tired or have symptoms. Do not start iron tablets without a test, because too much iron also causes harm. If you have haemochromatosis, avoid iron supplements.
