Bloating, gas, constipation and heartburn have different causes, so the right supplement depends on which problem you have. The [gut health hub](/en/supplements/gut-health/) lists every option with its evidence and checked products. This guide explains what the evidence says for the most common choices.

## Peppermint oil: the strongest evidence for belly pain and gas

The EMA's herbal committee concluded that peppermint oil taken by mouth can relieve minor belly spasms, flatulence and belly pain, especially in people with irritable bowel syndrome (IBS). Germany's S3 IBS guideline gives peppermint oil its strongest recommendation grade (A). The guideline refers to enteric-coated capsules.

Two limits to know. The EMA does not recommend these preparations for children under 8 for these uses. Heartburn and nausea are listed side effects, so check the label if reflux is your main problem.

## Psyllium (ispaghula husk): established for constipation

Psyllium forms a gel with water and works as a mild laxative. The EMA concluded it can treat habitual constipation and soften stools. In the EU it is regulated as a herbal medicine, not as a food with a bowel claim. Take it with plenty of liquid, and keep it at least half an hour to an hour apart from other medicines.

## Chicory inulin: the one authorised bowel claim here

Commission Regulation (EU) 2015/2314 authorises the claim that chicory inulin contributes to normal bowel function by increasing stool frequency. The authorised amount is 12 g a day. If you are not used to fibre, start with less and increase slowly, and drink enough water.

## Probiotics: possibly helpful, strain-dependent

A 2023 meta-analysis in *Gastroenterology* found low-certainty evidence that some probiotic strains help abdominal pain in IBS, and very-low-certainty evidence for bloating. The guidelines disagree on how far to go. The British Society of Gastroenterology's 2021 guideline lists probiotics as a first-line option and suggests trying them for up to 12 weeks. The American College of Gastroenterology's 2022 guideline does not support probiotics for overall IBS symptom relief. The German S3 guideline gives probiotics a general positive recommendation, but not grade A.

No EU-authorised health claim exists for probiotics and digestion. A product that promises to fix your gut is making a claim the EU register does not support.

## Digestive enzymes: little evidence for everyday bloating

Harvard Health's assessment is that there is little evidence for enzyme supplements against common digestive complaints such as heartburn. Trial evidence for enzymes is mostly in people with exocrine pancreatic insufficiency, a medical condition. For chronic pancreatitis, a Cochrane review found no definitive conclusion.

Lactase is the exception. It has an authorised EU claim for improving lactose digestion, which matters if dairy reliably upsets you. For other enzyme blends sold for bloating, the evidence is thin. If a doctor has prescribed enzymes, follow that prescription. It is a different decision from buying an enzyme capsule for bloating.

## What the EU lets labels say

- **Chicory inulin:** authorised claim for normal bowel function (Regulation 2015/2314).
- **Peppermint oil and psyllium:** traditional herbal medicine status in the EU, not food claims.
- **Lactase:** authorised claim for lactose digestion.
- **Probiotics:** no authorised digestion claim.
- **"Detox", "cleanse" and "heals your gut":** not authorised claims.

## A practical approach

1. Name the symptom and how often it happens.
2. Change one thing at a time, and give it several weeks. German S3 guidance notes that one to two weeks is too short to judge herbal medicines or probiotics.
3. Check interactions with your medicines, especially the timing of psyllium.
4. See a doctor for symptoms that persist or worsen, weight loss, or blood in the stool.
