## The short answer

For most people, a varied diet covers protein, and water covers fluid. Caffeine is the only ingredient here with a clear dose limit from EFSA. Carbohydrate-electrolyte drinks are the only one of these with an authorised EU claim, and it applies to **prolonged endurance exercise**, not to a normal gym session. Beta-alanine has no authorised claim.

## Protein: the EFSA reference value

EFSA's reference value for healthy adults, including older adults, is **0.83 g of protein per kg of body weight a day**. For 70 kg that is about **58 g**. This is a population reference, not a target for training. EFSA's page on the figure carries a notice that it is more than ten years old, so check the current EFSA opinion before you rely on it for a specific plan.

Protein from food counts towards this. For protein from everyday food, see [the best supplement is on your plate](/en/guides/best-supplement-is-on-your-plate/), and for strength training see the [creatine guide](/en/guides/creatine-monohydrate-guide/). Protein powder is a food product: the price per gram of protein is the number to compare, not the claims on the tub.

## Caffeine: what EFSA says

EFSA's 2015 safety opinion, summarised in its public lay text, reaches these conclusions for healthy adults:

* **Single dose:** up to **200 mg** (about 3 mg per kg of body weight) raises no safety concerns.
* **Per day:** up to **400 mg** spread through the day raises no safety concerns, except in pregnant women.
* **Pregnant and breastfeeding women:** up to **200 mg a day** from all sources is the level EFSA considers safe for the foetus.
* **Children and adolescents:** EFSA gives a per-kilogram figure only, and no milligram value for a child.

Caffeine doses in drinks and tablets vary widely, so read the label and add up all sources: coffee, tea, energy drinks, and pre-workout products. The 100 mg single dose is noted by EFSA as something that may affect sleep in some adults, so timing matters for recovery.

**Authorised claim:** none. In 2016 the Commission refused the claim "Caffeine helps to increase alertness", because EFSA did not establish a cause-and-effect relationship. A product that claims it boosts performance is making a claim the law does not allow.

## Electrolytes: which drinks can make a claim

The EU register authorises two claims for **carbohydrate-electrolyte solutions**, with conditions on the drink itself (from Regulation (EU) No 432/2012):

* "Carbohydrate-electrolyte solutions contribute to the maintenance of endurance performance during prolonged endurance exercise."
* "Carbohydrate-electrolyte solutions enhance the absorption of water during physical exercise."

The conditions are strict: 80–350 kcal per litre from carbohydrates, at least 75% of that energy from high-glycaemic sugars, and 20–50 mmol of sodium per litre (about 460–1,150 mg). A sports drink that does not meet them cannot use the wording.

A carbohydrate-only drink without electrolytes was refused its claim in 2016 (Regulation (EU) 2016/1379). We found no authorised EU claim for electrolyte tablets or powders on their own.

If you take diuretics, ACE inhibitors or other medicines that affect fluid balance or potassium, ask your doctor or pharmacist before using potassium-containing electrolyte products.

## Beta-alanine: no authorised claim

Beta-alanine is often sold for "increased performance in short, intense efforts". In 2016 the Commission refused the claim "Beta-alanine increases performance during short-duration high intensity exercise", because EFSA concluded that a cause-and-effect relationship had not been established. EFSA noted that only one of eleven relevant human studies showed an effect. Any label using that wording is making a claim the EU does not authorise.
